## General writing and document rules

- A proposal has three main chapters: Introduction, Literature Review and Methodology. Final documentation extends through Chapters 4–6.
- Use third person throughout the academic prose. Objectives retain infinitive verbs.
- For completed activities use past tense; for established knowledge, current system behaviour and what figures/tables show use present tense; reserve future tense for genuinely incomplete work. Update the abstract last.
- Use the supplied template for the cover, declaration/approval, acknowledgment, contents and figure/table/equation/abbreviation lists. Names, signatures and approvals must be genuine.
- The assessment requires Times New Roman, 12-point base text, 1.5 line spacing, appropriate heading/paragraph alignment, Roman preliminary numbering and decimal main-body numbering. Mathematical subscripts/superscripts may be proportionally reduced. Keep the number/caption convention consistent with the course/template.
- Use automatic heading/list numbering and regenerated contents/list fields. Verify page references in the final rendered PDF, including after Word/LibreOffice conversion.
- All figures and tables require readable labels, correct numbers, captions, appropriate in-text references and source credits where applicable. Preserve editable figure sources. Never conceal or bypass a watermark.
- Use tables for precise comparisons and figures for useful patterns, relationships or workflows. Decorative images and screenshots alone are not evidence of success.
- Keep editorial reviews, feedback matrices and pending actions in supporting files unless the course explicitly places them in the report.

## Topic and title

- Start from the intended contribution. The topic should be relevant, useful, feasible with the available data/resources/time, sufficiently focused, and grounded in current research.
- A gap's existence alone does not justify a study; explain its significance and the benefit of addressing it.
- Use a specific, concise title, normally **12–18 words**, in a single-title or main-title/subtitle form. Remove unnecessary phrases such as “An Approach to”, “A Study of” and “An Investigation of”. Retain meaningful technical keywords.
- A case-study title identifies the technology, the actual case/location/organization and the challenge or impact. Do not claim an institutional case study without the relevant access/evidence.
- Consult the supervisor about topic feasibility and scope. Never manufacture a meeting, decision or approval. Historic classroom due-date examples are not current project deadlines.

## Acknowledgment and abstract

### Acknowledgment

Formally acknowledge significant research-related contributions by individuals, institutions or organizations. Avoid unrelated personal dedications and unsupported claims about assistance.

### Abstract

- Present the background/problem, purpose/objectives, approach/method, actual or anticipated outcome and significance.
- Keep it self-contained, concise, readable and free of unnecessary technical detail, citations and equations.
- The general guide describes 100–250 words, usually about 200. **The specific marking scheme caps this proposal's abstract at 200 words.** Prefer a single coherent paragraph and meet the template's approximate half-page presentation without padding.
- Proposal-stage outcomes are anticipated; a completed-project abstract reflects actual methods, results, conclusions and contribution. Do not copy completed-result wording from the class example.
- Add keywords drawn from the abstract, formatted **bold and italic** as requested. Keep the abstract and keywords within the assessed length where feasible.
- Review organization, transitions, completeness, grammar, wordiness and consistency with the chapters. Rephrase naturally rather than copying source passages.

## Chapter 1: Introduction

### 1.1 Background Information

- Create a concise, evidence-supported narrative hook, then narrow toward the research problem.
- Explain relevant historical/technical developments and the disciplines involved.
- Establish what is known, identify an unresolved gap, explain why it matters and give the rationale for the study.
- State the guiding hypothesis/proposition or research questions without presenting anticipated success as established fact.
- Keep foundational citations relevant and rigorous. This is context, not an exhaustive literature review. Avoid unrelated history, excessive implementation detail, vague language and unverified local statistics.

### 1.2 Problem Statement

- Define the specific researchable problem and its consequences.
- Follow **ideal → reality/shortfall → consequences and research response**.
- Aim for approximately half a page or no more than about 250 words at this proposal stage. Cite the shortfall and relevant gap; quantify only where a source or measurement supports the number.
- Link the problem directly to the objectives and method. A claim that “no system exists” requires evidence; use a bounded gap that the reviewed studies support.

### 1.3 Research Objectives

#### 1.3.1 General Objective

One concise infinitive statement of the overall aim.

#### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives

- Concise action-oriented statements, **i., ii., iii., iv., v.** as needed, meeting SMART criteria: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.
- Use the course progression: analyse current state and challenges; study existing contributions; identify limitations/gaps; design/develop the solution; test/validate it.
- The number of objectives depends on scope; the active NANFO revision uses five to preserve this progression.
- Put measurement detail in the methodology where appropriate. Do not make objectives lengthy catalogues or repeat the same task under different numbers.
- The section consists only of the numbered statements, without a mapping table or explanatory paragraph.

### 1.4 Research Questions

Derive each question directly from its objective, retaining the same order, scope and terminology. Use the matching lowercase Roman list. Questions must be answerable by the planned research and must not presuppose favourable outcomes.

### 1.5 Justification

Connect current knowledge, missing evidence, the practical/academic problem it causes and how the proposed study helps. Identify beneficiaries and plausible benefits. Do not invent improvement percentages, institutional costs or first-of-kind claims.

### 1.6 Scope

State the technical area/method, data sources and boundaries, included functions, excluded functions and timeframe. The course work plan concludes by **the end of November 2026**, with presentations before final exams in reading week. See the separately recorded original-Gantt override above.

### 1.7 Limitations and Delimitations

Separate unavoidable constraints from intentional boundaries:

- **Limitations:** available resources, access, missing records, sample size, shared-host timing, representativeness and other threats to validity. Explain their likely effect and mitigation actually undertaken.
- **Delimitations:** deliberate choices of environment, technologies, tasks, data range, users and excluded areas that keep the study manageable.

## Chapter 2: Literature Review

### Purpose and critical method

- Survey and critically evaluate relevant scholarly/official sources, showing their contribution to the problem and relationships among studies.
- Synthesize themes, methods, findings, conflicts, alternative explanations and unresolved gaps. Avoid an unconnected list of supportive summaries.
- Assess provenance/evidence quality, methodological appropriateness, objectivity, persuasiveness and value. Vendor publications support product functions, not independent comparative effectiveness.
- Use evidence and careful paraphrase; keep the analysis selective and in third person. Do not include irrelevant sources merely to grow the bibliography.

### 2.1 Introduction

Restate the problem briefly, explain the review's purpose, selection criteria and thematic layout, and indicate how the sections align with the objectives. Prefer work from the previous **three years**, with **five years** as the broader current-research window. Primary protocol or algorithm definitions may be older when essential, but do not use them as a substitute for current research evidence.

### 2.2 Current State and Challenges

Explain the relevant field, current approaches, reported successes and specific challenges in cited prose. It corresponds to analysis of the current state/challenges. Merge genuinely related objectives only where this improves flow without losing traceability.

### 2.3 Related Works

For each selected system or implementation, explain its purpose/context, technology, method/data, reported findings, evidence limitations and implications for the project. At least **three systems must be cited** to satisfy the assessment; use a consistent comparative table where useful. Screenshots are optional and must add information, not replace critical analysis.

### 2.4 Gaps in Related Works

Write connected **prose paragraphs**, with citations to the reviewed works and cross-references to relevant sections/tables. Derive a defensible unmet need from their limitations. Avoid unsupported absolute novelty claims and bullet-point fragments copied from the classroom example.

### 2.5 Conceptual Framework

- Identify research focus, inputs, processing variables and outcomes.
- Explain the theoretical basis, expected relationships and feedback/control mechanisms.
- Link the design/development objective to the processing elements and the validation objective to measurable outputs.
- Present a readable figure and explain its relationships. Refinement may follow later testing, but a proposed arrow does not prove a capability is implemented.
- NANFO's assessed conceptual image earned full marks and is retained unless the student expressly requests a change. Explain implementation interpretation accurately.
